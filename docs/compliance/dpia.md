# Aptly — Data Protection Impact Assessment

**Controller:** Joao Perracini (individual; Aptly is a non-commercial student project, not a company)
**Contact:** contactaptly@gmail.com
**Version:** 1.1 — 15 September 2026
**Status:** Processing update for diagram-aware assessment; controller review pending. The conclusions accepted on 18 August 2026 have not been reassessed or approved for this change.

This update records implementation facts for the feature-flagged diagram-aware
pilot. It does not constitute controller approval, a new compliance conclusion or
evidence of teacher calibration. Existing legal analysis below is retained for
review alongside the changed processing.

A DPIA is required here, not optional. The ICO's Article 35(4) list requires one for
"the use of the personal data of children … for … profiling or other automated
decision-making, or if you intend to offer online services directly to children",
and Standard 2 of the Children's Code requires one independently. Aptly does both:
it is offered directly to school students and it profiles their academic performance
using AI.

Kept proportionate to what Aptly actually is — a free study tool with a small number
of users, no revenue and no staff.

---

## 1. What Aptly is

Aptly is a web app that helps IB Economics students practise answers. A student
pastes or types a question and answer, or uses a saved Practice question. With the
diagram-aware pilot enabled, a relevant diagram and explanation can be assessed
together, including a diagram-only submission. Aptly returns an estimated mark
and feedback, and over time it shows which topics and skills are weakest and what
to practise next.

It is free. There is no paid tier, no advertising, no marketing, no sponsorship and
nothing for sale. It is not operated through a company.

**Users.** Realistically 13–18 year olds studying the IB Diploma, plus some adult
teachers or self-studiers. Children are not an edge case — they are the core user
group, and the assessment below assumes that throughout.

**Scale.** Small. A handful of real student and test users at the time of writing.

## 2. What is processed

| Category | Examples | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Account | email, generated user ID, nickname, SL/HL level | Given by the student |
| Student work | questions, answers, pasted source material, revisions | Given by the student |
| Photo bytes | handwriting scans, diagram photos | Given; transient requests/browser memory, not persisted by Aptly on the server or in sessionStorage |
| Assessment output | estimated marks, feedback, strengths, improvements, mistake types, syllabus topic, diagnostic breakdown, diagram observations and interpretations | Model-derived decisions; combined totals and applicable ceilings reconciled by the server |
| Assessment binding/provenance | private contract snapshot, question/context/answer hashes, image content hashes/roles, model/reviewer versions | Generated by the server; saved with completed combined attempts; no image bytes |
| Derived profile | weakest/strongest topics, recurring mistakes, Current Focus, Study Next, estimated performance | Inferred by Aptly |
| Technical | timestamps, session, quota/idempotency records, failure logs | Generated by the system |

No special-category data is deliberately processed. Nothing is collected about
health, beliefs, ethnicity or anything similar. See risk R4 for the incidental case.

**The derived profile is not stored.** It is recomputed in the browser from saved
attempts on every page load (`lib/assessment/readiness.ts`). The source attempts,
their assessments and, for completed combined assessments, private immutable
snapshots and visual observations are persisted. Snapshot records cascade-delete
with their attempt or account. The private snapshot is not browser-readable.

## 3. Necessity and proportionality

Every category above is needed for something the student actively asked for:

- Email — the only way to sign in (magic link). No password to leak.
- Nickname and course level — the greeting, and gating HL-only questions.
- Answers and feedback — the service itself.
- History — the Learning log, progress and Current Focus are all built from it.
- Quota records — Aptly pays per AI call; without a cap one user could exhaust it.

Nothing is collected "just in case". There is no analytics SDK, no advertising, no
tracking, no geolocation, no device fingerprinting, no contact list, no social graph.

Where minimisation was possible it was taken: photo bytes are not persisted on
Aptly's server, image metadata is stripped before upload, quota records hold no
content, and failure logs record an error *class* and a pipeline stage but never
the message, the student's
work, their email or their user ID.

An attached diagram can remain in browser memory for an immediate revision. The
student explicitly retains, replaces or removes it; reopening after reload needs
reattachment. Observations and hashes saved with the assessment have the attempt's
retention lifecycle. They are retained records even though the original photo is
not. Provider-side retention is a separate consideration (§5).

**Lawful bases** are in `operations.md` §2. In short: contract for the service
itself; legitimate interests for security and quota enforcement. Consent is not
relied on anywhere, which matters for a child-facing service — a 14-year-old cannot
meaningfully consent to processing that the product cannot function without, so
consent would have been the wrong and weaker basis.

## 4. Best interests of the child

Required by Standard 1 of the Children's Code. Assessed honestly rather than
asserted:

**Where Aptly serves a student's interests well.**
- It exists to help them learn, and gives feedback most students cannot otherwise get.
- Nothing is public. There is no sharing, no profiles, no leaderboards, no social
  comparison and no way for one student to see another's marks. A weak result cannot
  embarrass them.
- No advertising, so no incentive to maximise time-on-app or harvest attention.
- No teacher or school visibility, so practising badly carries no consequence. That
  is deliberate: a student should be able to fail safely.
- Daily caps incidentally limit compulsive over-use.
- Marks are consistently labelled estimates, so the product does not encourage a
  student to over-trust it.

**Where a student's interests could be harmed.**
- A wrong mark could mislead revision or knock confidence (R1).
- A "weakest topic" judgement is a statement about a child's ability, and being told
  you are weak at something is not neutral (R2).
- Streaks mildly encourage daily return (R6).

**Conclusion.** On balance the design serves students' interests, and the main
residual risks are about AI accuracy and tone rather than data exploitation. The
mitigations in §6 target exactly those.

## 5. Who else is involved

| Provider | Role | What it gets |
|---|---|---|
| Supabase | Processor — auth + database + magic-link email | Everything stored |
| OpenAI | Processor — grading, transcription, diagram assessment/review, generation | Question, answer, source text, photos and visual observations. **No account name, email or user ID supplied by Aptly**; student work may itself contain personal details. |
| Hosting provider | Processor — serves the app | Request data in transit, platform logs |

`store: false` is set on every model request, including the combined visual
observation and authoritative assessment stages, so OpenAI is asked not to store
the response. Under its current API terms, API content is not used to train its models by
default. OpenAI's own security/abuse monitoring may still hold limited records — the
Privacy Notice says so rather than overclaiming.

Transfers outside the UK occur (OpenAI is US-based). Providers' published terms apply
the UK Addendum to the standard contractual clauses. Confirming regions and executing
the DPAs are founder actions — see `operations.md` §9.

## 6. Risks and mitigations

**R1 — AI gives a wrong mark or wrong feedback.** *Likely to happen occasionally;
moderate impact.* A student could revise the wrong thing or lose confidence over an
unfairly harsh mark.
Mitigations, all already in the product: marks are labelled practice estimates
throughout and never as IB grades; confidence tiers and "Low evidence" labels appear
wherever a judgement is thin; a mark total that cannot be established produces
feedback-only rather than a guessed number; diagnostic bars are labelled diagnostic
and never summed into the headline mark; the generator rejects any output claiming to
be official; grading provenance (model, rubric version, taxonomy version) is stamped
on every row so a bad output can be traced; and a student can delete any answer,
which removes it from every insight. Copy-audit tests pin this wording so it cannot
silently regress.

**Diagram-aware processing change.** For a supported four-mark diagram question,
the question-specific diagram and explanation components are actual marking
components, distinct from diagnostic bars. The server computes their total and
applicable ceilings. Longer answers use the diagram as evidence within the
existing holistic framework, without a separate bonus. Visual observations can
still be wrong, especially where labels, image quality or teacher annotations
are ambiguous. The implementation separates observations from interpretation.
For an attached image, essential evidence must be readable and the student region
certain before a completed score is saved. Confirmed omission is distinct from
unreadable evidence. Unreadable essential evidence produces no saved scored attempt.
These controls and mocked technical tests do not establish grading accuracy;
teacher calibration remains pending.

**Residual:** The August 2026 low-moderate acceptance covered the earlier product.
The controller must review the changed visual grading risk before recording an
updated residual rating or acceptance.

**R2 — Profiling a child's academic ability.** *Certain to happen; low-moderate
impact.* Aptly labels topics as weak and names recurring mistakes.
Mitigations: it exists only to direct the student's own revision; it is never shared
with schools, teachers, parents or other students; it is not stored as a profile but
recomputed from deletable source data; evidence thresholds prevent a confident claim
from one or two answers; and the Privacy Notice explains it in plain language.
Crucially, it has **no external effect** — see R3.
**Residual: low.**

**R3 — Automated decisions with a real-world effect.** Assessed and **not engaged**.
UK GDPR Articles 22A–22D apply to a *significant* decision — one with a legal or
similarly significant effect. An Aptly mark determines nothing: not a school grade,
not a predicted grade, not university admission, not access to education, not a place
in a set. Nobody but the student ever sees it. The ICO's example of a similarly
significant effect on a child is one affecting "health or access to education", and
Aptly affects neither.
**This conclusion depends on positioning** — see review triggers.

**R4 — Sensitive information typed into a free-text box.** *Possible; potentially
high impact.* A student writing a real-world example could mention a name, a school,
a family circumstance or a health matter. A scan captures a whole page unreviewed.
Mitigations: the Submit screen warns "Don't include personal information"; the
Privacy Notice repeats it; photo bytes are not persisted by Aptly on the server,
but extracted text and visual observations can persist with an attempt. An image
may therefore disclose incidental details even without a saved photo. Students
should avoid including those details and can delete affected attempts or their
account; associated snapshots cascade-delete. Access is private by default.
Not deliberately processed, so no Article 9 condition is claimed — the control is
minimisation and warning.
**Residual:** The previous low-moderate acceptance needs controller review for
retained visual observations; this update does not record renewed acceptance.

**R5 — Unauthorised access to another student's work.** *Unlikely; high impact.*
Mitigations: row-level security enforces per-user access at the database, not in
application code; browser roles hold permitted SELECT/DELETE access to their own
attempts/Practice, with INSERT/UPDATE revoked entirely; private assessment snapshots
have no browser grants; all authoritative writes run server-side under a
service-role key that is server-only by build-time guarantee; route protection
verifies the JWT rather than trusting a session; column-level grants hide grading
blueprints from the browser; and a security verifier proves cross-user isolation
against a local instance.
**Residual: low.**

**R6 — Nudge techniques.** Streaks and "Keep it going today" encourage return visits.
They push *practice*, not data disclosure or weakened privacy, which is what Standard
13 targets. No pressure to share more, connect accounts or reduce settings exists.
**Residual: low. Keep streak copy supportive; avoid guilt or loss framing.**

**R7 — Indefinite retention.** Addressed in this release: quota/idempotency rows are
swept after 30 days. Study history is deliberately kept while the account exists,
because it is what the product is for, and the student can erase all of it by
deleting their account.
**Residual: low.**

## 7. Age assurance: why not

Standard 3 offers two routes — establish age with certainty proportionate to the
risk, **or** apply the standards to all users. Aptly takes the second, deliberately.

Age verification would mean collecting a date of birth, an ID document, or a
third-party age estimate. That would add *more* sensitive personal data about
children than the entire rest of the product holds, to a free study tool that carries
no advertising, no adult content, no purchases, no sharing and no contact between
users. The risks the standard is designed to catch are absent here, so the intrusion
would be disproportionate.

Instead: a stated minimum age of 13 (aligning with UK GDPR Article 8 and OpenAI's API
terms), no date of birth collected, and **every protection applied to every user
regardless of age**. Private by default, no advertising, no sharing, no selling, no
profiling for advertising, plain-language transparency, and self-service deletion.

## 9. Review triggers

Redo this DPIA before shipping any of the following. Each would change a conclusion
above, and several would reopen R3.

- **Teacher, parent or school access** to a student's work or profile — changes the
  best-interests analysis, the sharing position, and very likely R3.
- **Positioning marks as predicted or official grades** — would likely make them
  significant decisions and engage Articles 22A–22D.
- **Payments or subscriptions** — brings consumer law in, and puts the e-commerce
  service-provider disclosure duty (including a geographic address) beyond doubt.
  That duty is already fact-sensitive rather than settled at the current scale —
  see `operations.md` §9 item 12.
- **Advertising of any kind** — would contradict Standards 5, 12 and 13.
- **Analytics or tracking technology** — reopens the cookie/PECR conclusion.
- **Sharing, social or comparison features** — reopens the best-interests analysis
  and could bring the Online Safety Act into scope, which currently does not apply
  precisely because no sharing functionality exists.
- **Accepting users under 13** — changes the Article 8 position and, under OpenAI's
  under-18 guidance, requires zero data retention first.
- **A different AI provider, or materially different retention terms** — §5 and R1
  both rest on the current terms.
- **Persisting raw image bytes on Aptly's server or in browser storage**, or
  expanding their current transient request/in-memory use — changes the
  minimisation and retention assumptions.
- **Material changes to how diagrams affect marks or to retained visual
  observations** — the current diagram-aware change is recorded above and remains
  pending controller review.
